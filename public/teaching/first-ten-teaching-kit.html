From conversations to a next move

Instructor guide · 60 minutes

First Ten teaching kit 1.0 · October 18, 2026. Suitable for pairs or groups of three; individuals can use a self-check. No account, AI, paid service or outside contact is required. This is a fictional classroom exercise, followed by an optional one-week field assignment.

Prepare: give students paper or an accessible document. Keep the structured worksheets and model reasoning hidden until after their first thinking. At minute 10, show only the StudyLoop source table and tasks; cover or omit the facilitator section until students choose. Provide the rubric in advance; arrange private submission and accessible alternatives. A timer and shared display are optional. Do not ask students to put personal information in the fictional exercise.

Practice objectives: distinguish a source from an interpretation; write a question about recent behavior; use counterevidence to explain a choice; connect an audience, channel, truthful asset and useful action; revise after feedback. These are teaching objectives, not measured learning gains.

Opening prompt: “A student built a campus study-partner app. Before you see anyone else’s plan, what would you need to find out, decide or do next? Write your own notes and organize them in a way you could use.” Do not supply the answer or ask for a positive conclusion.

Teaching built into the workflow

  • Experiential learning: rehearse a decision here; use a permitted real action in the field assignment.
  • Generative, student-led thinking before AI: preserve the original board before adding prompts or AI.
  • Scaffolded practice: reveal questions and checks after students create their own structure.
  • Formative peer feedback: observe comprehension and an attempted next action; revise.
  • Metacognition and reflection: explain what changed, what did not and what remains uncertain.
  • Responsible AI and human judgment: keep AI optional; students review claims and own decisions.

Facilitation: ask “Which source supports that?” and “What else could explain it?” Accept a defensible decision to investigate further, retain a direction or stop. Score reasoning, not agreement with the model answer. No posting, contacting real groups or generating fake evidence occurs in this class.

Access: allow typing, handwriting, dictation to a chosen scribe, oral explanation or an accessible plain-text copy. Offer extra processing time by reducing the number of comparison examples, not the reasoning criteria. A solo self-check can replace a peer when pairing is inaccessible; label it honestly. No grade depends on AI use, network size, customers or spending.

StudyLoop: what changes the next move?

Fictional case · no real participants or results

A student’s starting idea is a campus app for finding study partners. Their starting assumption: “Students struggle to find enough people to study with.” These four perspectives are invented teaching material, not a customer dataset, testimonial or proof of demand.

Source IDFictional materialSource type
studyloop-maya“I don’t need more people. I need to know who’s actually showing up.”Fictional direct quote
studyloop-owenDescribes repeated last-minute messages to work out who will attend a study session.Fictional paraphrased note
studyloop-priyaDescribes an existing group that works well when attendance is clear.Fictional paraphrased note
studyloop-noraAlready has a reliable study group and does not want another app.Fictional counterexample; paraphrased note

Give students these tasks

  1. Select a source. State what it says without adding a cause, frequency or market size.
  2. Write one tentative interpretation and another possible explanation.
  3. Explain how Nora changes the audience assumption, if at all.
  4. Write one question about a recent actual situation that could distinguish the explanations.
  5. Choose a next move and explain what could change your mind.

Facilitator reasoning example · reveal after students choose

Observation: Maya describes uncertainty about attendance; Owen describes coordination messages. Priya’s group works when attendance is clear. Nora reports a working routine and no desire for another app.

Interpretation: coordination may matter more than finding more people for some existing groups. Alternative: a clearer agreement in the group’s existing chat may be sufficient. These excerpts do not establish how common either situation is or whether a new tool would help.

One defensible decision: focus on existing groups with unreliable attendance and test a simple confirmation flow before building a discovery feed. Asking more groups, retaining the original idea while seeking missing evidence, or stopping can also be reasoned choices.

Proposed experiment, not run: invite five consenting groups with this problem to try confirmations for one week and compare each group’s own attendance records with its previous routine. Observe use, missed confirmations and workarounds. No result, payment, improvement rate or completed recruitment is supplied. An uncontrolled small test cannot isolate a causal effect.

Past-behavior question: “Tell me about the last study session when attendance was uncertain. What happened, and how did your group handle it?” Follow with “What happened next?” rather than “Would you use our app?”

Fictional marketing continuation

Consider a campus-club organizer introduction and a relevant digital study-group space. Exact venue/link, rules and access are unknown; do not invent them. Select a primary and alternative route for investigation.

Sample organizer request: “I’m testing a simple way for existing study groups to confirm attendance. Does your group have this problem? If appropriate, may I show the idea?” This is a fictional draft to critique, not a message to send. The destination and useful action must be defined before any real outreach.

Discussion: What evidence justifies the audience? What permission is missing? What would a recipient actually do? What observation would lead you to repeat, revise or stop? All counts remain unknown.

One-week assignment · build, reach, learn

Your app, two reachable routes, one small test

Use a working app/prototype and your own permitted observations. The objective is an explained decision, not a customer quota. $0 means no new cash spending: time, existing AI allowance, equipment, printing, travel and access still have limits. No purchase or public disclosure is required.

Submit privately: original notes/structure; audience/evidence/unknowns; one neutral question about recent behavior; two-channel plan; one reviewed asset; tasks/resource limits; source-and-date measurement log; and a 200-300-word reflection or equivalent 2-3-minute oral/accessible response. Use the field worksheet or your own structure with the same information. The instructor states the actual dates, private submission route and contact for alternatives before assigning this work.

DayStudent actionEvidence to retain
1Think alone before templates/AI. Name the audience and useful action, what is known and what is assumed.Original notes and a permitted source; “unknown” where needed.
2Investigate specific physical/digital places; choose primary and alternative. Check rules, access and resource needs.Exact place/link, observation/date, permission status and fallback.
3Make one truthful channel-specific asset. Connect asset → destination → useful action. Have a peer or self-check try the path.First version, observed comprehension/action and revision.
4-6If allowed and feasible, perform a small manual test. Log tasks and observations. Stop if permission/access fails.Actual action/date, source, counts or unknown; support/reminders.
7Review the evidence and decide repeat, revise or stop.Reflection citing sources, limits, changed/retained decision and next step.

Before outreach: truthful feature/claim, builder relationship disclosed, destination works, useful action clear, community/venue permission checked, participant choices respected, and a realistic plan for responding. No unsolicited repeated messages or automatic sending. Being a classmate, enrolled student or known contact is not permission for every use.

People and data: ask permission before taking notes. External AI processing and sharing an excerpt require separate choices for the actual material and audience. Keep personal/contact details out of submissions; do not upload customer/student notes to AI by default. Stop or use the alternative if the relevant permission is absent. Classroom work is not automatically First Ten product research.

AI, optional only after your own plan: use permitted non-sensitive context, ask for a bounded draft, inspect unsupported claims and note what you changed. Example prompt: “Using only my supplied audience, constraints and channel observations, suggest questions I should investigate. Do not invent URLs, permissions, quotes or results.” Use your existing allowance or stay manual; do not subscribe or spend to meet the assignment.

Equivalent accessible/private alternative: use StudyLoop, inspect a public source without contacting people, and rehearse an asset/destination with a consenting peer or alone. Label everything simulated, proposed or observed in rehearsal. Leave field outcomes unknown and reflect on what would need checking. If you lack an app, access, time, a network or permission, this route earns the same rubric opportunities. An unrun test reported honestly is preferable to invented evidence.

Reflection prompts: What did I predict? What did I observe? Which source or disagreement affected my decision? What came from me, a peer, an instructor or AI? What is still unknown? What will I repeat, revise or stop, and why? Include course reminders/help; activity required for a course is not voluntary product retention.

Assessment · reasoning and participant respect

Four criteria, 0-3 each · 12 points total

Use for the case, fieldwork or private alternative. A well-reasoned stop or honestly unrun test can earn full credit. Explain expectations in advance. No automated grading or authenticity score is supplied.

Do not grade: customer/revenue totals, network, spending, AI use, polish, positive validation or agreement with the instructor. Respect alternative formats and nondisclosure. A failed test can show sound reasoning; a successful count can have weak evidence.

Feedback: one supported source/decision, one uncertainty, one next action the student can choose. Ask for an explanation before assuming misconduct. Correct fabricated data through established institutional processes; this worksheet cannot verify that an interview occurred.

Pedagogical rationale and limits

The owner’s BUS131 “Fail Faster, Fail Smarter,” Week 6 slides inform this sequence: own structure before AI (pp. 4-5), audience/places (6-8), two routes and measures (12-14), tasks/resources (16-17), peer walkthrough/revision/reflection (20-24). This is curriculum provenance, not an effectiveness study.

Patricia Greene’s Four Approaches to Teaching an Entrepreneurship Method (Babson, February 14, 2024) discusses practice, design and reflection. It informs the lesson’s rationale; it is not endorsement or evidence of First Ten’s efficacy.

The EEF’s Metacognition and Self-Regulated Learning (second edition, November 13, 2025) is school-level guidance on planning, monitoring and evaluation. Applying it here is a design inference; do not transfer school attainment effects to university entrepreneurs or this app. Sources checked October 18, 2026.
