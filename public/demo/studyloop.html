StudyLoop: when the first assumption changes.

Fictional demo data · No customer research or results

All people, quotes and notes below are authored teaching examples. No experiment has run. This text is available without the interactive controls or JavaScript.

The starting idea

Help students find study partners.

Assumption: Students struggle to find enough people to study with.

Four perspectives

Maya · Fictional quote

“I don’t need more people. I need to know who’s actually showing up.”

Owen · Fictional note

Describes repeated last-minute messages to work out who will attend a study session.

Priya · Fictional note

Describes an existing group that works well when attendance is clear.

Nora · Fictional counterexample

Already has a reliable study group and does not want another app.

A possible interpretation

Attendance coordination may matter more than finding more people for some existing groups.

Another explanation: These groups may need a clearer agreement, not another app.

Still unknown: We don’t know how common this is or whether a tool would help.

Nora’s counterexample challenges a broad audience of all students. Ask what differs between reliable groups and groups with uncertain attendance.

Choose a next move

In the main story, the founder narrows the audience to existing groups with uncertain attendance and proposes testing confirmations before building a discovery feed. These are three possible next moves, not scored answers.

1. Test attendance confirmations

Focus on existing groups with uncertain attendance. This tests the coordination interpretation without assuming that every student needs another app.

Proposed · Not run · No outcome recorded

Question
Would a simple confirmation flow be useful for these groups?
Small next action
Invite five consenting groups with this problem to try it for one week; compare each group’s own attendance records with its prior routine.
What to observe
Whether groups use it, missed confirmations, attendance and workarounds.
What could change the decision
Groups ignore it, their existing chat works as well, or coordination improves without a new app.
Still unknown
Whether any change would persist or result from the confirmation flow.

2. Ask another group

You can investigate the disagreement before building. Look for how a reliable group organizes itself and how an uncertain group differs.

Proposed · Not run · No outcome recorded

Question
What explains the contrast?
Small next action
Speak with one group with reliable attendance and one with uncertainty about their most recent session.
What to observe
Their existing agreements, reminders and actual attendance.
What could change the decision
The problem is not recurring or has a simpler non-product solution.
Still unknown
How widespread either situation is.

3. Keep exploring partner discovery

You can retain the original direction, but these excerpts do not yet support it. Look for people who recently tried and failed to find suitable partners.

Proposed · Not run · No outcome recorded

Question
Who has an unmet partner-finding problem?
Small next action
Seek recent concrete attempts and current workarounds before building the feed.
What to observe
What was tried, who was available and what blocked a useful match.
What could change the decision
Existing ways of finding partners already meet the need.
Still unknown
Whether a discovery feed would be used.

Existing study groups with uncertain attendance

Nora’s reliable group is not the target. A better agreement may be enough; a new app is only one possibility.

Fictional student thinking: Start with a club where people already study together. Ask its organizer whether attendance is a recurring difficulty before offering a trial.

One useful action: A suitable group voluntarily agrees to discuss a one-week confirmation trial.

Sample draft: Campus club introduction

Hello [organizer], we’re students exploring study-group routines. We’re exploring a simple attendance-confirmation idea with existing study groups. It is an early student project; we have not shown that it improves attendance. Does this fit your club? If so, may we invite interested members to a conversation? Please do not share anyone’s contact details without their permission. Suggested invitation, only if allowed: Does your study group spend more time checking who’s coming than getting started? If attendance is a recurring problem for your group, would you be open to a short conversation about trying confirmations for one week?

Proposed useful-action measurement: Count unique groups that voluntarily agree to discuss the proposed trial; keep participant counts separate. A conversation is not adoption.

Repeat: Repeat a small invitation only if the intended groups understand it and describe a recurring problem. This would still not establish demand.

Revise: Revise if the invitation attracts the wrong groups or people mistake an early idea for a proven product.

Stop: Stop this route if access is refused, the problem is absent, or a simpler existing routine already meets the need.

One group with reliable attendance and one with uncertainty

Nora’s reliable group is a comparison to learn from, not a sales prospect. Explore what already works without asking it to switch.

Fictional student thinking: Find contrasting routines before offering a product. An organizer might introduce two willing groups; permission and interest are unknown.

One useful action: A willing group agrees to describe its most recent study session.

Sample draft: Campus club introduction

Hello [organizer], we’re students exploring study-group routines. We’re comparing how study groups organize their sessions, including groups whose routines already work well. This student research invitation is not a product launch. Does this fit your club? If so, may we invite interested members to a conversation? Please do not share anyone’s contact details without their permission. Suggested invitation, only if allowed: What helps your study group know who will turn up? Would your group describe its most recent session and how you coordinated attendance in a short conversation? It is fine to decline.

Students who recently tried and failed to find suitable study partners

The four fictional excerpts do not yet support this direction. Nora does not need another app; seek a relevant recent attempt instead.

Fictional student thinking: Find a recent unsuccessful partner search before making a discovery feed. Ask in a relevant course community only if its rules allow research invitations.

One useful action: A student with a recent unsuccessful search volunteers to describe it.

Sample draft: Campus club introduction

Hello [organizer], we’re students exploring study-group routines. We’re exploring how students find study partners. If this happened recently, would you share what you tried and what got in the way in a short conversation? There is no product to sign up for.
